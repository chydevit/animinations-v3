## 1. The name: 扶南 Funan

- **Characters / readings.** 扶南, Mandarin *Fúnán*. Middle Chinese (Baxter): *bju‑nom*; Pulleyblank: *buə̆‑nəm*; Karlgren: *bʱi̯u‑năm*. Vietnamese: *Phù Nam*. Khmer: ហ្វូណន (also spelled ហ៊្វូណន) and the Khmer patriotic name **នគរភ្នំ** ("kingdom of the mountain").
- **The name appears only in Chinese sources.** No local inscription uses "Funan". We do not know what the people called their own country. **[DOCUMENTED]** (Vickery 2003; Jacques 1979)
- **"bnaṃ / phnom = mountain" etymology** **[HYPOTHESIS → DEBATED]**:
  - *Coedès*: 扶南 transcribes Old Khmer *bnaṃ/vnaṃ* (modern *phnom*, "mountain"). Finot added that its kings were titled *kurung bnaṃ*, "king of the mountain".
  - *Claude Jacques* argued this rested on a mistranslation of the Sanskrit *parvatabhūpāla* ("mountain king") in much later Angkorian inscriptions. He showed there is no evidence for a "king of the mountain" title.
  - *Vickery (2003: 125–126)*: we know Funan rulers' titles only as *Hun*, *Fan* and *‑varman*. Also, in Chinese place names for the south, 南 *nan* normally means literally "south" (南海 "Southern Sea", 安南 "Pacified South", 日南 "South of the Sun"). So *Fu‑nan* might be a Chinese descriptive name, e.g. Aymonier's "protected south". Pelliot thought that possible but preferred a transcription, because some texts write the first syllable with other characters.
  - Vickery also notes that the modern Khmer name *នគរភ្នំ* (*mahānokor bhnaṃ*) "implies a much larger area than most historians of Cambodia would accept".
- **Safe wording (EN):** "The Chinese called it *Funan*. Many Cambodian scholars link the name to the Old Khmer word *bnam*, 'mountain' – which is why it is called Nokor Phnom – but historians still debate whether that is right."
- **Safe wording (KM):** «ចិនហៅនគរនេះថា "ហ្វូណន"។ អ្នកប្រាជ្ញខ្លះយល់ថា ឈ្មោះនេះមកពីពាក្យខ្មែរបុរាណ "វ្នំ" ដែលមានន័យថា "ភ្នំ" ហេតុនេះហើយបានជាយើងហៅថា នគរភ្នំ — ប៉ុន្តែអ្នកប្រវត្តិវិទូនៅតែជជែកគ្នាអំពីរឿងនេះ។»
- **Title of film:** "Kingdom of the Mountain" is fine as the *traditional Khmer name*. The film should say once, early, that it is a traditional name and not a proven translation.

**Reliability problems to state in the film (briefly):**
1. **Late compilation.** The most detailed texts (*Liang shu*, *Jin shu*) were finished in 636–648, long after Funan's peak. They copy earlier reports, sometimes garbled. (Vickery 2003, n. 22)
2. **Chinese perspective.** Funan is described as a "tributary" bringing "tribute". These are Chinese diplomatic categories. In practice the missions were trade and diplomacy.
3. **Transliteration.** Local names survive only as Chinese sound‑spellings (混填 Hun Tian, 范師蔓 Fan Shiman, 闍耶跋摩 Sheyebamo = Jayavarman). Some can be matched to Sanskrit (‑跋摩 = *‑varman*); most cannot.
4. **Story layers.** Legend (Hun Tian), oral history (Fan dynasty), and observation (Kang Tai) are mixed in the same chapters. (Vickery 2003: 105)
5. **Prejudice.** Physical descriptions ("ugly and black") are the Chinese observers' judgment, not a neutral description (see §6).

## 3. Before Funan (keep brief)

- **Angkor Borei (Takeo):** first occupied by the **late 5th or early 4th century BCE**. Three consistent sets of radiocarbon dates from separate parts of the site support this. **[ARCHAEOLOGY]** (Stark, LOMAP; Stark 2003/2006)
- **Vat Komnou cemetery** (centre of Angkor Borei): excavated 1999–2000. At least 45 burials were recovered (some summaries say c. 60). The cemetery was used **c. 200 BCE – 200 CE**; some sources extend this to 400 CE. Grave goods include pots, animal offerings and beads. **[ARCHAEOLOGY]** (Stark LOMAP; Pietrusewsky analyses)
- **Oc Eo area:** Vietnamese archaeologists define a **"Pre‑Oc Eo" phase (3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE)**, an **Early Oc Eo phase (1st–3rd c. CE)** and a **Developed Oc Eo phase (4th–7th c. CE)**, based mainly on radiocarbon dates. **[ARCHAEOLOGY]** (Bui Van Liem et al. 2018, summarised in Nguyen H. B. L. 2023)
- **Gò Tháp (Đồng Tháp):** a long‑lived sacred site in the Plain of Reeds. It has early brick and wood foundations, gold‑leaf foundation deposits, wooden Buddhas, and inscription K.5 (5th c.). **[ARCHAEOLOGY]** Precise phase dates were not verified here.
- **Wider Iron Age context:** delta settlements grew rice, worked metal, lived with seasonal floods, and traded across the Gulf of Thailand (e.g. Khao Sam Kaeo on the Thai‑Malay peninsula, 4th–2nd c. BCE). Funan grew out of these local communities, roughly 500 years after Angkor Borei was founded. **[ARCHAEOLOGY / HYPOTHESIS]** (Stark: "social complexity emerged gradually, over a 500‑year period")
- **Safe line:** "Long before Chinese writers ever heard of Funan, people were already living, farming and burying their dead at Angkor Borei – from around 400 BCE."

## 4. Founding legend(s) — all **[LEGEND]**

### 4a. Chinese versions (oldest → latest)

Pelliot and Vickery count four Chinese versions with different names. **None calls the hero "Kauṇḍinya", and none names the queen "Somā".**

1. **Kang Tai, *Wushi waiguo zhuan*** (3rd c.; preserved in *Taiping yulan* 347, 983 CE):
   扶南之先，女人爲主，名柳葉。有模趺國人，字混愼，好事神……夜夢人賜神弓一張，教載賈人舶入海。混愼晨入廟，于神樹下得弓……神回風令至扶南，柳葉欲劫取之，混愼舉神弓而射焉。貫船通度，柳葉懼伏。
   — "The ancestor of Funan was a woman ruler named **Liu Ye** ('Willow Leaf'). A man of the country of **Mofu**, called **Hun Shen**, was devoted to a god… At night he dreamed someone gave him a divine bow and told him to board a merchant ship and go to sea. In the morning he went to the temple and found the bow under the sacred tree… The god turned the wind so that he reached Funan. Liu Ye wanted to plunder [the ship]; Hun Shen raised the divine bow and shot – the arrow went right through the boat. Liu Ye was afraid and submitted."
2. ***Jin shu* 97** (648): the queen is **葉柳 Ye Liu** and the man **混潰 Hun Kui** (Golzio reads 混湏 Hunhui), "a foreigner who served a god". The story has the dream, the bow found at the shrine, a voyage with merchants, and Ye Liu's surrender. Hun Kui "took her as wife and occupied her country. Later his descendants declined… and his general Fan Xun again ruled Funan." **No cloth episode.**
3. ***Nan Qi shu* 58** (c. 537): queen **柳葉 Liu Ye**; **混填 Hun Tian** "from the country of Ji 激國". Dream of a bow → found under the temple tree → sailed to Funan → Liu Ye led her people to resist → "Hun Tian raised the bow and shot from afar; it pierced one side of the boat and struck a man inside" → Liu Ye surrendered and married him. **"Disliking that she went naked, he folded cloth and put it over her head"** (惡其裸露形體，乃疊布貫其首). He ruled and his descendants succeeded him.
4. ***Liang shu* 54** (636): adds that Funan people "originally went naked, tattooed their bodies and let their hair hang loose". Liu Ye was "young and strong, like a man". Hun Tian came from **徼國 / 激國** "to the south". The arrow "pierced one side and reached an attendant". He "taught Liu Ye to pierce cloth and pass her head through it, so her body was no longer exposed" (教柳葉穿布貫頭，形不復露). They "had sons who were each given one of **seven towns** (分王七邑)". A later king **Hun Pankuang 混盤況** used intrigue to set the towns against each other and conquered them.

**Interpretation [DEBATED]:**
- *Pelliot & Coedès*: Hun Tian = **Kauṇḍinya**, an Indian brahmin, which made this the "first Indianisation". 
- *Vickery 2003: 106–108*: nothing in the Chinese versions says the stranger came from India or was a brahmin. His home (Mofu/Ji/Jiao) was probably somewhere in the southern seas or the Malay Peninsula. The Chinese, who transcribed Indic names carefully, would not have rendered the three syllables *Kau‑ṇḍi‑nya* as two. Pelliot himself thought the episode could not be later than the 1st c. CE.
- *Golzio 2023*: agrees. Hun Tian is called a brahmin only in the 10th‑c. *Taiping yulan*, and "it is completely out of place to connect that couple with the later one" (Kauṇḍinya–Somā).
- *Manguin 1991 (via Vickery)*: the story resembles other Southeast Asian "stranger‑king from overseas" founding myths.
- **Film approach:** tell the bow‑and‑boat story as the Chinese told it. Say clearly: "Later traditions gave the hero an Indian name, Kaundinya."

### 4b. Kauṇḍinya and the nāgī Somā — from **Cham and Khmer inscriptions**, not Chinese

- **Mỹ Sơn stele C.96** (Champa), of King Prakāśadharman (Vikrāntavarman I), **dated 18 February 658 CE**, stanzas XVI–XVIII. **[INSCRIPTION]**: "Kauṇḍinya, the foremost of brahmins, obtained the spear of Droṇa's son **Aśvatthāman** … and planted it in the town called **Bhava[pura]**". He then married **Somā, daughter of a king of serpents** (*bhujagendra*). (Finot 1904: 918–925; Golzio 2023)
  - *Golzio 2023*: C.96 sets the couple at **Bhavapura**, the 6th–7th c. capital of Bhavavarman I. Inscription K.1142 makes their son Candravarman marry the granddaughter of Īśānavarman (c. 616–637). On that reading the inscription treats them as **7th‑century dynastic ancestors** of a Chenla line, not as founders of Funan in the 1st c. **[DEBATED]**.
  - The planted spear and the nāga‑princess motif parallel the **Pallava** dynasty legends of South India (Aśvatthāman + nāga princess).
- **Angkorian inscriptions (10th c.)**, e.g. **K.286 (Baksei Chamkrong, 948 CE)**: King Rājendravarman claims descent from "Rudravarman and the kings born of **Kauṇḍinya and the daughter of Soma**". Here Somā is the **daughter of Soma (the Moon)**, *not* a serpent princess. Golzio notes that Coedès's index added "nāgī" by mistake. **[INSCRIPTION]**
- **K.5 (Gò Tháp, 5th c.)** is the only Funan‑period inscription to mention the name: Guṇavarman is "a moon of the lineage of Kauṇḍinya". **[INSCRIPTION]**
- **Chinese "Kaundinya II"**: the *Liang shu* calls a 4th/5th‑c. king **憍陳如 Qiaochenru** (= Kauṇḍinya) and says he was a brahmin from India (see §5). Vickery reads this as Funan rulers adopting the Indian Kauṇḍinya clan legend after two centuries of contact with India, not as a real immigrant. **[DEBATED]**

### 4c. Preah Thong (ព្រះថោង) and Neang Neak (នាងនាគ)

- A Khmer folk legend: the prince Preah Thong marries the nāga princess Neang Neak (often identified with Somā). The nāga king drinks up the sea to create land for them. The story is linked to the traditional wedding custom of the groom holding the bride's sash (*sbai*). Place names near Angkor Borei (Kok Thlok district, a "Preah Thong" reservoir) are associated with it (Stark LOMAP). **[LEGEND]**
- **[DEBATED] – age of the legend.**
  - *Vickery 2003: 101*: neither name appears in any Funan document. *Thong* is a Thai word ("gold") that could not have entered Khmer before the 14th c. at the earliest. The tale is probably a Cambodian adaptation of Ayutthaya's U‑Thong legend, i.e. **post‑Angkorian**.
  - *Golzio 2023*: "there is no trace of Kauṇḍinya‑Somā in the later folk‑tales of Cambodia". Preah Thong and the nāgī should be distinguished from historical persons.
  - *Popular view (and some museum and NGO texts, e.g. Stark's LOMAP summary, Ledgerwood)*: one continuous story from Funan to today. **Do not repeat the claim that Chinese visitors heard the Preah Thong story** – they heard of Hun Tian and Liu Ye.
- **Film approach:** present the three couples as **three different stories from three eras**:
  1. Hun Tian & Liu Ye – Chinese records, told to 3rd‑c. envoys.
  2. Kauṇḍinya & Somā – inscriptions, 7th c. (Cham) and 10th c. (Angkor).
  3. Preah Thong & Neang Neak – later Khmer folk tradition, still alive in weddings.
  Vickery notes these are often blended into one "folk‑historical jumble". Showing them separately is both accurate and a good teaching moment.

## 6. Kang Tai 康泰 and Zhu Ying 朱應

- **Who:** Kang Tai held the post of 中郎 (Palace Gentleman) and Zhu Ying was 宣化從事 (Assistant Officer for Propagating [Imperial] Civilisation), envoys of the **Wu** kingdom (capital Jianye/Nanjing). The mission came after Wu's governor of Jiaozhou, **Lü Dai 呂岱**, "sent officers south to spread [Wu's] civilising influence, and the kings of Funan, Linyi and Tangming beyond the frontier each sent envoys with tribute" (*Sanguozhi* 60; before Lü Dai's recall in **231**). **[DOCUMENTED – Sanguozhi, c. 280s–290s]**
- **Date [DEBATED]:** Pelliot first put it c. 225–230, then settled on **245–250** (Pelliot 1903: 292–293, 303; followed by Vickery). Others link it to Lü Dai's activity (226–231). The *Liang shu* says the envoys went to **Fan Xun's** country and met the Indian envoys Chen and Song, who had come back with Fan Zhan's mission. That favours the 240s. **Safe:** "in the mid‑3rd century" / «ពាក់កណ្តាលសតវត្សទី៣».
- **What was reported** (exact source in brackets):
  - Walled towns, palaces and houses: "有城邑宮室" [*Jin shu* 97]
  - Farming: "sow once, harvest three years" (一歲種，三歲獲) [*Jin shu* 97]
  - Love of carving; eating vessels mostly of silver; **taxes/tribute paid in gold, silver, pearls and perfumes** (貢賦以金銀珠香) [*Jin shu* 97]
  - **Books, archives and treasuries; "their script resembles that of the Hu"** (亦有書記府庫，文字有類于胡) [*Jin shu* 97]. *Hu* 胡 is the general Chinese term for peoples to the west and north‑west, including Central Asians and Indians. It most likely means an Indic (Brahmi‑derived) script. **[HYPOTHESIS, widely accepted]**
  - Nakedness and the cloth: "the people still went naked, only women wore a garment with a head‑hole"; the envoys' remark → Fan Xun ordered men to wear the 橫幅 wrap‑cloth, "today's *ganman*" (干漫). The rich cut brocade for it; the poor used plain cloth [*Liang shu* 54].
  - The king's palace pavilions, receiving guests "three or four times a day", gifts of bananas, sugar cane, turtles and birds; **ordeals** (red‑hot axe carried 7 steps; gold ring or egg pulled from boiling water); crocodiles in the city moat and wild beasts outside the gate "to test the guilty" [*Liang shu* 54, under Fan Xun]
  - Houses: "the largest houses are carved and engraved; [people] like giving alms; the king likes to hunt, all ride elephants, away a month at a time" [*Wushi waiguo zhuan* fragment, *TPYL* 786]
  - Theft ordeal by chewing rice at a shrine [*Waiguo zhuan*, *TPYL* 786]
  - **Houses on stilts / raised houses:** the explicit statement is in ***Nan Qi shu*** 58: "伐木起屋，國王居重閤…人民亦為閣居" ("they fell trees to build houses; the king lives in multi‑storey pavilions… the people also live in raised [pavilion] houses"), roofs thatched with giant leaves 8–9 chi long. The *Liang shu* uses 閣 / 干闌 *ganlan* (stilt house) for **Linyi** and adds "all the countries from Linyi and Funan southwards are like this" (自林邑、扶南以南諸國皆然也) – that clause in context covers clothing and customs. **[DOCUMENTED – Nan Qi shu, c. 537]** **[ARCHAEOLOGY]**: Gò Thành (Tiền Giang) has a collapsed thatched house with wooden beams, ¹⁴C‑dated to the 4th c.; Oc Eo has pile foundations (Nguyen H. B. L. 2023).
  - **Physical description:** "人皆醜黑拳髮，倮身跣行" (*Jin shu*) / "今其國人皆醜黑，拳髮" (*Liang shu*): "the people are all ugly and black, with curly hair; they go naked and barefoot". **Present this only as the prejudiced view of foreign observers.** Recommendation: do **not** put this line on screen as a quotation. If it is mentioned at all, narrate: "Chinese writers described the people with the prejudices of their time – for example, judging dark skin as 'ugly'. Such descriptions tell us more about the observers than about the people of Funan." The *Nan Qi shu* notes that in neighbouring Linyi "people consider black beautiful; so it is in all the southern countries" (人色以黑為美，南方諸國皆然).

## 7. Oc Eo (Óc Eo; Khmer អូរកែវ)

- **Location:** Óc Eo, Thoại Sơn area, **An Giang Province, Viet Nam**, at the foot of **Ba Thê mountain** (Núi Ba Thê). Site centre c. 10.233 N, 105.152 E. The present‑day country is Viet Nam; describe it neutrally ("in today's southern Viet Nam"). Do not frame it in terms of modern territorial claims. Many Cambodians call the area Kampuchea Krom; if this is mentioned, say neutrally that Khmer communities have lived in the delta for centuries.
- **Discovery and excavation:** identified through aerial photography and surveys (Pierre Paris, late 1920s–1930s; Malleret). **Louis Malleret** began prospecting in **1942** (excavation began 10 Feb 1942 according to some sources) and carried out his main campaign **Feb–April 1944**, at about 12 locations. The campaign ended with the Japanese coup of March 1945. Results: *L'archéologie du delta du Mékong*, 4 vols, **1959–1963**. **[ARCHAEOLOGY]**
- **Size:** Malleret mapped a rectangular enclosure c. **3,000 × 1,500 m ≈ 450 ha**, with a long central canal and cross‑canals. Today's protected heritage area is **433–453 ha** (Ba Thê zone + Oc Eo plain; Viet Nam News 2026). **[ARCHAEOLOGY]**
- **Dates:**
  - Vietnamese phasing (Bui et al. 2018): pre‑Oc Eo 3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE; early 1st–3rd c.; developed 4th–7th c.; post‑Oc Eo 8th–10th c.
  - Malleret's view: a port of Funan in the 2nd–6th c.
  - Thermoluminescence dating of bricks suggests occupation 1st–8th c. (cited in coverage of Wang, Hung et al. 2023).
  - **Safe:** "flourished from about the 1st to the 7th century CE, at its height roughly the 2nd to 6th centuries".
- **Finds** **[ARCHAEOLOGY]**:
  - Gold jewellery, rings (some with bull motifs or Sanskrit letters), gold foil; moulds and tools for jewellery → **local workshops**.
  - **Seals and intaglios** with short Brahmi/Sanskrit inscriptions (personal names) in carnelian and other stones.
  - **Glass and stone beads, including evidence of local bead production** (Oc Eo was famous for "uncountable beads").
  - **Tin and lead‑tin amulets and ornaments**: Malleret named it a "tin culture".
  - **Roman‑linked items:** two gold pieces imitating coins/medallions of **Antoninus Pius (r. 138–161)** and, less certainly, **Marcus Aurelius (r. 161–180)**. *Borell 2008/2023*: these are **jewellery pendants imitating Roman coins**; moulds elsewhere in the region prove local production. A Vietnamese study argues they were actual coins. **[DEBATED]** The often‑quoted "152 CE" date (from a tribunician year on the Antoninus piece) is **[UNVERIFIED – do not narrate]**. Say "made in the time of the Roman emperor Antoninus Pius, mid‑2nd century".
  - Roman‑style bronze keys (2nd–3rd c.), Roman‑style mosaic eye‑beads, **Eastern Han bronze mirror(s)** and ***wuzhu* coins** (1st–3rd c.), Indian fine pottery (1st–6th c.), Chinese ceramics (2nd c. onward). Persian/West Asian items (bronze lamps, beads) are reported in Bui et al. 2018/2022 as summarised by Nguyen H. B. L. 2023. "Sasanian coins" are **[UNVERIFIED]**.
  - Local silver coins with a *hamsa*/rising‑sun motif (Malleret; attribution debated).
  - **Spices (2023):** starch grains on grinding stones show turmeric, ginger, galangal, sand ginger, fingerroot, clove, nutmeg and cinnamon. One slab is radiocarbon‑dated **207–326 CE** from charcoal beneath it. The authors call it the earliest evidence of curry‑making in Southeast Asia; the "curry" label is their **interpretation** (Wang, Hung et al., *Science Advances* 2023).
  - Religious: brick temples (Gò Cây Thị, Linh Sơn, Nam Linh Sơn Tự), Viṣṇu statues, Buddha images, liṅgas. The large Phnom‑Da‑style Viṣṇu of Ba Thê is 6th–7th c.
- **"Cattigara":** Malleret (and Dihle, Hanus & Smagur 2020) identified Oc Eo with Ptolemy's *Kattigara*. **[HYPOTHESIS]** — say "possibly".
- **Holding institutions:** **Museum of Vietnamese History, Ho Chi Minh City** (main Malleret collection, also K.5 stele); **National Museum of Vietnamese History, Hà Nội**; **An Giang Museum** (Long Xuyên); Óc Eo Culture exhibition house at the site. A Musée Guimet (Paris) Oc Eo holding is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **Status:** National Special Monument of Viet Nam (2012). On UNESCO's tentative list (2022); full nomination submitted Jan 2026; ICOMOS field evaluation Aug 2026; decision hoped for at the 49th World Heritage Committee (2027). **Do not say it is a World Heritage Site.**

## 8. Angkor Borei and the canals

- **Angkor Borei (Takeo; អង្គរបុរី)**, c. 10.99 N, 104.97 E, on the western edge of the delta. **[ARCHAEOLOGY]** (Stark LOMAP since 1996):
  - Walled settlement enclosing **at least 300 ha** at its peak, with brick and earth walls and moats inside and outside. The wall circuit is c. 6 km (UNESCO tentative‑list description).
  - **More than a dozen brick monuments.**
  - Founded 5th/4th c. BCE; Vat Komnou cemetery c. 200 BCE–200 CE.
  - Local museum (EU‑funded) holds sculpture, inscriptions and artefacts.
  - Home of **K.557/600**, the earliest *dated* Khmer‑language inscription (611 CE; §14).
  - Possibly one capital of "Funan": Aymonier and Pelliot favoured Angkor Borei; Coedès favoured Ba Phnom. Vickery notes that the new archaeology of Angkor Borei, and the absence of comparable remains at Ba Phnom, "may force a change". **[DEBATED]**
- **Canals:**
  - French aerial photography (1920s–1930s; **Pierre Paris 1931, 1941**; then Malleret) revealed **long straight ancient canals** across the Transbassac.
  - One links **Oc Eo to Angkor Borei, c. 70 km**.
  - Malleret's **Lung Lớn canal** runs **over 100 km**, Angkor Borei → Oc Eo → Nền Chùa near the old coast (Nguyen H. B. L. 2023, after Malleret 1959). A Vietnamese team re‑mapping from aerial and satellite data counts c. 28 canals.
  - A total "≈200 km of canals" is often repeated but **[UNVERIFIED – do not narrate an exact total]**. Say "a network of canals, some running for tens of kilometres".
- **Dating a canal: Bishop, Sanderson & Stark 2004** (*J. Archaeol. Sci.* 31: 319–336; the actual title is "OSL and radiocarbon dating of a pre‑Angkorian canal in the Mekong delta, southern Cambodia"; the companion paper is Sanderson et al. 2003, *Quaternary Science Reviews*). **[ARCHAEOLOGY]**
  - Canals near Angkor Borei were mapped from air photos and tested by augering and trenching.
  - OSL places the digging (or re‑digging) of the canal bed **between the 1st millennium BCE and the mid‑1st millennium CE**.
  - Pooled charcoal radiocarbon dates of the **early 4th–early 5th c. CE** probably mark when the canal **began to silt up**.
  - The authors call the chronology tentative. They warn that the canal's demise "does not indicate depopulation".
  - The canal number ("canal 15") was not verified.
- **Purpose [HYPOTHESIS]:** transport between inland centres and the coast, drainage, and perhaps irrigation. Holcombe, Stark and others take them as evidence of organised, centralised labour.

## 9. Fan Shiman's expansion

- **What the texts say** **[DOCUMENTED – Chinese record]**:
  - *Liang shu* 54 (636): Fan Man (范蔓) "was brave and had strategic skill; again by force of arms he attacked neighbouring states, all of which submitted. He styled himself 'Great King of Funan' (扶南大王). He had great ships built, crossed the whole Swelling Sea, attacked **Qudukun 屈都昆, Jiuzhi 九稚, Diansun 典孫** and more than ten states, and opened up 5–6,000 *li*. Next he was to attack **Jinlin 金鄰**, but fell ill and sent his crown prince Jinsheng in his place."
  - *Nan Qi shu* 58 (c. 537) gives the name **范師蔓 Fan Shiman** but does not list the conquests.
  - The *Liang shu* also says Funan's realm was "over 3,000 li around" and that **頓遜 Dunsun** (= Diansun), "3,000 li to the south, on a peninsula", had **five kings, all subordinate to Funan** (並羈屬扶南). Dunsun is described as a busy market where "east and west meet, over 10,000 people a day".
- **Identifications [HYPOTHESIS]:**
  - **Dunsun:** on the **Thai‑Malay Peninsula**, probably the isthmus region (Pelliot, Wheatley *Golden Khersonese* 1961). Vickery reports a Mon etymology, "five cities", and the *Liang shu* says its language was "a little different from Funan's".
  - **Jinlin 金鄰** ("Golden Frontier"): often linked to *Suvarṇabhūmi* and placed in lower Burma or central Thailand.
  - **Qudukun, Jiuzhi:** unidentified (Pelliot); Wheatley offered speculative peninsular locations.
- **How much is claim vs evidence [DEBATED]:**
  - Coedès took these as a real maritime "empire".
  - Boisselier even suggested Funan's centre was in the Chao Phraya basin.
  - Vickery accepts some Funan presence along the coast to the peninsula, but rejects Fan Shiman = Śrī Māra of the Võ Cạnh stele, and with it any Funan rule as far as Nha Trang.
  - Archaeologically, shared material culture (beads, seals, jewellery, Oc Eo‑type pottery) across the Gulf of Thailand shows **trade networks**, not necessarily political control (Hà Văn Tấn).
  - "5–6,000 li" (very roughly 2,000–3,000 km) is a conventional Chinese figure. **Do not convert it into a map border.**
- **Safe line:** "Chinese records say Fan Shiman built big ships and made more than ten coastal states submit – perhaps as far as the Malay Peninsula. We cannot know exactly how far his power really reached."

### 10c. Religion

- **Śaiva** **[DOCUMENTED – Nan Qi shu, c. 537]**: Nāgasena told the Qi court (484) that Funan "worships the god **Maheśvara** (摩醯首羅 = Śiva), who constantly descends on **Mount Modan** (摩躭山)". Jayavarman's memorial also contains long verses praising the **Buddha and bodhisattva path**: Śaiva and Buddhist themes in one royal letter.
- **Vaiṣṇava** **[INSCRIPTION]**: K.5 (Viṣṇu footprint), K.875 (Viṣṇu on Śeṣa); Phnom Da statues (Viṣṇu, Kṛṣṇa, Balarāma).
- **Buddhist** **[INSCRIPTION / DOCUMENTED]**: K.40. A coral Buddha image sent 503; a sandalwood "auspicious image" and palm‑leaf texts sent 519; the Buddha‑hair claim 539; Funan monks translating in China (§12). Wooden Buddhas of the delta (§11).
- ***Liang shu*** **[DOCUMENTED, 636]**: "they worship the gods of heaven; the gods are images of bronze, those with two faces have four arms, those with four faces eight arms, each hand holding something – a child, a bird or beast, or the sun and moon" (俗事天神，天神以銅為像，二面者四手，四面者八手).
- **Royal ‑varman names** (Jayavarman, Rudravarman, Guṇavarman): Sanskrit "armour/protector". The fashion is documented from the 5th c. onward. Earlier rulers bear titles transcribed *Hun* and *Fan*.
- **Kaundinya II** ("used the laws of India") – see §5; interpretation **[DEBATED]**.

## 11. Society, art and daily life

**From Chinese texts** **[DOCUMENTED]**:
- **Clothing:** men wrap a horizontal cloth (橫幅), "now called ***ganman*** 干漫" (cf. a sampot‑like wrap; the comparison with the modern *sampot* is **[HYPOTHESIS]**). The wealthy use brocade, the poor plain cloth; women wear a garment with a head‑hole (貫頭) [*Liang shu*; *Nan Qi shu*].
- **Hair and body:** "originally went naked, tattooed, hair loose" — a statement about the legendary past [*Liang shu*].
- **Houses:** timber houses; the king's multi‑storey pavilion; wooden palisade walls; roofs of giant leaves; people live in raised houses [*Nan Qi shu*]. "Several dozen families share one pond; they do not dig wells" [*Liang shu*].
- **Boats:** 8–9 zhang long, 6–7 chi wide, fish‑shaped bow and stern [*Nan Qi shu*].
- **Metalwork:** "they forge gold rings, bracelets and silver eating vessels" (鍜金鐶鏆銀食器) [*Nan Qi shu*].
- **Slaves and trade:** "Funan people are clever and skilful; they raid neighbouring towns that do not submit, taking people as slaves, and trade for gold, silver and coloured silks" [*Nan Qi shu*].
- **Entertainment:** **cockfighting and pig fighting** (鬪鷄及狶為樂) [*Nan Qi shu*; *Xin Tang shu*: 人喜鬥雞及豬].
- **Elephants:** the king rides an elephant; "women can also ride elephants" [*Nan Qi shu*].
- **Justice and ordeals:** no prisons. A gold ring or egg is thrown into boiling water and must be pulled out; a red‑hot chain or axe is carried seven steps; diving tests; the guilty are burned, the innocent unharmed [*Nan Qi shu*; *Liang shu*]. Crocodiles and wild beasts test the accused (Fan Xun's time) [*Liang shu*].
- **Music:** in 243 Fan Zhan sent **musicians** to Wu [*Sanguozhi* 47]. Detail on instruments is not given.
- **Funerals:** four kinds – in the river, cremation, burial, and exposure to birds; mourners shave hair and beard [*Liang shu*].
- **Taxes:** in gold, silver, pearls and perfumes [*Jin shu*]; gold, pearls and perfumes [*Xin Tang shu*].
- **Character:** "by nature good, not fond of war; often attacked by Linyi" [*Nan Qi shu*] vs. "greedy… without rites" [*Liang shu*]. Both are Chinese moral judgments; **do not narrate either as fact**.

**From archaeology** **[ARCHAEOLOGY]**: bead‑making and jewellery workshops with moulds (Oc Eo); tin amulets; pile‑built houses along canals; brick temples; imported and local pottery; spices; gold‑leaf foundation deposits (Gò Tháp, Gò Thành).

**Art — the Phnom Da style:**
- **Eight monumental Vaiṣṇava sculptures from Phnom Da** (hill just south of Angkor Borei, with the artificial caves and the **Asram Maha Rosei** stone shrine).
  - **National Museum of Cambodia (MNC):** Harihara (Ka 1614), **Kṛṣṇa Govardhana (Ka 1641)**, Kṛṣṇa Govardhana from Vat Koh (Ka 1625), Balarāma(?) (Ka 1608), Rāma (Ka 1638), **eight‑armed Viṣṇu (Ka 1639)**, Balarāma (Ka 1640).
  - **Cleveland Museum of Art:** Kṛṣṇa Govardhana (1973.106).
  - **Musée Guimet (Paris):** the **Harihara of Asram Maha Rosei** (taken by Aymonier, 1883).
  - (Porte 2016)
- **Dating [DEBATED]:**
  - *Pierre Dupont (1955)*: "Phnom Da Style A" under **Rudravarman (c. 514–539)**, i.e. "Funan art".
  - *Bertrand Porte (2016)*: same workshop, "**second half of the 6th century, under Rudravarman, last king of Funan**". Note the tension with Rudravarman's documented dates (514–539+).
  - *Nancy Dowling (1999, Asian Perspectives 38)*: **mid‑7th century** (ornament band and double‑lotus base), i.e. **after** Funan, in the Chenla period. This "shortens by 100 years the chronology for early Cambodian sculpture".
  - *Paul Lavy (2003, JSEAS 34)*: follows Dowling. The Asram Maha Rosei Harihara "should no longer be seen as the earliest Preangkorian image" of Harihara; Harihara images belong to 7th‑c. politics (Īśānavarman).
  - *Boisselier* (late): 7th–8th c.
  - Museums vary: Cleveland c. 600; others 6th–8th c.
  - **Safe:** "Phnom Da sculptures – 6th or 7th century; historians disagree whether they belong to Funan's last years or to the early Chenla period."
- **Wooden Buddhas of the delta:** standing Buddhas carved in *Calophyllum* (mù u) wood, preserved in waterlogged soil, from Gò Tháp, Giồng Xoài, Phong Mỹ, Nhơn Thành, Bình Hòa and elsewhere. Dated **4th–6th c.** in Vietnamese studies (Nguyen H. B. L. 2023), and **some reportedly radiocarbon‑dated**. The specific Dowling radiocarbon results were **[UNVERIFIED]**; check Dowling and Lê Thị Liên before giving numbers. Holdings: Museum of Vietnamese History HCMC; Đồng Tháp, An Giang and Long An museums.

## 12. Last kings: Jayavarman and Rudravarman

- **Jayavarman's 484 memorial** **[DOCUMENTED – Nan Qi shu 58, c. 537]**:
  - Background, "at the end of the Song" (before 479): Jayavarman, "whose surname is Qiaochenru (Kauṇḍinya)", sent merchants to Guangzhou. The Indian monk **Nāgasena 那伽仙** sailed back with them, was blown to Linyi and robbed, and reached Funan overland. He told the king that "China has a sage ruler who received Heaven's mandate".
  - In **Yongming 2 (484)** Jayavarman sent Nāgasena with a memorial. Its contents:
    - Formal good wishes to the emperor, crown prince and court.
    - The robbery story.
    - A complaint that "my slave **Kiu‑chou‑lo 鳩酬羅** ran away, broke Linyi and made himself king" — the king asks Qi to send troops against Linyi and offers to help.
    - A Sanskrit‑style hymn about **Maheśvara descending on Mount Modan** and long verses on the bodhisattva path and the Buddha.
    - A gift list: a **gold‑worked seated image of the nāga king (金鏤龍王坐像)**, a white sandalwood image, two ivory stūpas, two pairs of cotton cloth, two glass (lapis?) vessels, a tortoiseshell areca tray.
  - The emperor praised him but declined to send an army, and replied with silks.
- **504 title** **[DOCUMENTED – Liang shu 2]**: "Kauṇḍinya Jayavarman" made **"General Pacifying the South, King of Funan"** (安南將軍、扶南王).
- **Death 514** [*Liang shu* 54: after the embassies of the 10th and 13th Tianjian years (511/514), "that year he died"].
- **Rudravarman** **[DOCUMENTED – Liang shu 54, 636]**: "son of a secondary wife (庶子)… killed his legitimate younger brother and made himself king" (殺其嫡弟自立). Embassies 517, 519, 520, 530, 535, **539**.
  - **539**: presented a **live rhinoceros**, and "said his country had a hair of the Buddha twelve *chi* long". Emperor Wu sent the monk **Shi Yunbao 釋雲寶** with the envoys to welcome it.
  - **K.40** confirms Rudravarman son of Jayavarman. **K.53** shows his physicians' family continuing to serve Chenla kings.
  - Murdered brother = Guṇavarman? **[HYPOTHESIS – Coedès; doubted by Vickery]**.
- **Funan monks in Liang China** **[DOCUMENTED – Xu Gaoseng zhuan 1, 645]**:
  - **Saṅghapāla 僧伽婆羅** ("Sengqiepoluo", Chinese meaning "Saṅgha‑nourished"/"Saṅgha‑armour"), "a man of Funan". Born c. 460, died **524** aged 65 (普通五年). Came by ship to the Qi capital and studied under Guṇabhadra. **Translated 506–518 at five places in Jiankang (incl. the "Funan Hall" 扶南館)**: 11 works in 48 juan, including the ***Ayuwang jing* (Aśoka sūtra, T2043)** and ***Jietuo dao lun* (Vimuttimagga, T1648)**. Emperor Wu attended the first translation session in person. Other attributions (e.g. *Wenshushili wen jing* T468, *Kongque wang zhou jing*) follow later catalogues; check the Taishō catalogue before listing.
  - **Mandra 曼陀羅 (Mandrasena; 曼陀羅仙)**, "a Funan monk" who arrived early in the Liang (503) with many Sanskrit texts. With Saṅghapāla he translated the ***Baoyun jing* 寶雲經 (Ratnamegha)**, ***Fajie tixing* 法界體性** and ***Wenshu bore jing* 文殊般若經** (= T232), 3 works in 11 juan. "He did not master Chinese well, so his translations are obscure."
  - Later: the Funan monk **Subhūti 須菩提** translated the *Mahāyāna Ratnamegha* (8 juan) for the Chen. And **Paramārtha** came to China (546) at Funan's arrangement.

## 13. Decline and Chenla

- ***Sui shu* 82 (636)** **[DOCUMENTED]**: "Zhenla 真臘, south‑west of Linyi, was **originally a vassal of Funan**… its king, of the *kṣatriya* clan, is named **Zhiduosina 質多斯那 (Citrasena)**; from his ancestors on it gradually grew strong, and under Citrasena it **annexed Funan**. When he died his son **Yishenaxian 伊奢那先 (Īśānasena = Īśānavarman)** succeeded, residing at **Yishena city 伊奢那城 (Īśānapura = Sambor Prei Kuk)**, with over 20,000 households". Zhenla's embassy to Sui: **616** (大業十二年).
- **Inscriptions [INSCRIPTION]:**
  - **Bhavavarman I** (late 6th c.; Sharan 580–598; Coedès c. 550): inscriptions incl. K.213 (Phnom Banteay Neang). Linked with Bhavapura. Parentage debated (Vīravarman per the Wat Ban Khwao inscription). Coedès's idea that he was a grandson of the Funan king is undermined by Jacques.
  - **Citrasena‑Mahendravarman** (c. 600–c. 615): about 20 short victory and foundation inscriptions from the Mun valley to Champasak and Sa Kaeo (Revire 2016). Brother of Bhavavarman.
  - **Īśānavarman I** (c. 616–637): **Sambor Prei Kuk**; dated inscriptions 616/627 etc.
  - **K.53** links service from Rudravarman to these kings.
- ***Xin Tang shu* (1060)** **[DOCUMENTED, very late]**: the Funan king ruled from **特牧 Temu** (Coedès: = *Vyādhapura* "city of the hunter", near **Ba Phnom**, Prey Veng; others reject this, since Old Khmer *dalmāk* = "trapper"). It was "soon annexed by Zhenla", and the court "moved further south to **那弗那 Nafuna**" (Coedès: *Naravaranagara*, perhaps Angkor Borei; Malleret even suggested Oc Eo). **[HYPOTHESIS for both identifications]**. The same text says the Chenla king 剎利伊金那 (Īśāna) "**at the beginning of Zhenguan [627–]** annexed Funan" — this contradicts the *Sui shu* (Citrasena). **[DEBATED]**
- **Conquest vs gradual change [DEBATED]:**
  - *Coedès*: northern Chenla vassal conquered Funan in the late 6th–early 7th c. Some later writers add "Khmer Chenla vs non‑Khmer Funan".
  - *Vickery (1998, 2003)*: the idea of a rapid Khmer descent from the north "belongs to the realm of scholarly myths". Most early Khmer inscriptions are from the **south** (incl. Angkor Borei, 611) and spread northward through the 7th c. Funan and Chenla are Chinese labels for shifting centres of one Khmer‑speaking world. Mahendravarman's "conquests" may have been "exploratory probes".
  - *Jacques (1979)*: "Funan" and "Zhenla" are Chinese views concealing a reality of many small polities.
  - *Revire, Stark*: shift in where power sat rather than a single conquest.
  - Note that the Chinese still received "Funan" embassies in 559–588 and 618–649.
- **Maritime causes [HYPOTHESIS]:**
  - *Wolters (1967)*, *Hall (1985/2011)*: from the 5th–6th c., ships increasingly sailed directly between the Malacca/Sunda straits and China, bypassing the Funan coast and isthmus portages. Sumatran and Javanese ports benefited; later **Śrīvijaya** (late 7th c.) dominated.
  - Environmental change in the delta (canal silting from the 4th–5th c.; Bishop et al. 2004) is another suggested factor; the authors themselves warn against reading it as depopulation.
  - **Safe:** "Trade routes changed, new centres rose inland, and by the 7th century Chinese writers spoke of 'Zhenla' instead of 'Funan'."

## 14. Legacy: what can safely be said

- **Language:** "In the absence of any writing in a local language, the identification of the language of Funan is not possible" (Vickery 2003: 125). **[DEBATED]**
  - *Khmer (Mon‑Khmer)*: Vickery; continuity at Oc Eo in ceramics and stratigraphy (Manguin 2002, cited by Vickery).
  - *Austronesian (Malayo‑Polynesian), at least in the ports*: Hall, Thurgood, many Vietnamese scholars (e.g. Mạc Đường).
  - Port cities were certainly multi‑ethnic.
  - **Safe:** "We don't know for certain what language Funan's people spoke; many historians think most were ancestors of the Khmer, while its ports were home to many peoples."
- **Earliest dated Khmer text:** **K.557/600, Angkor Borei, Śaka 533 = 611 CE**. It is in Old Khmer and lists servants, rice fields and animals given to a temple. **[INSCRIPTION]** (Jenner 1980; English translation in *Vostok/Oriens*). The earliest Khmer‑language inscriptions cluster in the **south**, in former "Funan" territory.
- **Continuity of the elite:** K.53 (667/8): one family served Rudravarman and then the Chenla kings. **[INSCRIPTION]**
- **Royal ideology and memory:**
  - The "‑varman" naming, Sanskrit court culture and Viṣṇu/Śiva/Buddha patronage continue into Chenla and Angkor.
  - **Angkorian kings claimed Funan ancestry**: K.286 (948) names Rudravarman and Kauṇḍinya–Somā as ancestors of Rājendravarman's line. K.549 (Phnom Da, later) remembers Rudravarman.
- **Water management and sacred hills** (Phnom Da, Ba Thê) prefigure later Khmer practice. **[HYPOTHESIS]** (Bourdonneau 2007, "Réhabiliter le Funan: Óc Eo ou la première Angkor", argues strongly for continuity.)
- **Safe closing line:** "Funan was not the Angkor Empire, and it may not have been a single kingdom at all. But its ports, canals, temples and royal traditions are among the deepest roots of Cambodian civilisation."

### 15c. How to draw Funan's extent

- **There are no known borders.** The Chinese give only "over 3,000 li around" and rhetorical conquest figures.
- Draw a **soft glowing core** over the **lower Mekong delta and the Bassac/Takeo–An Giang plain** (Angkor Borei – Oc Eo – Gò Tháp – Ba Phnom). Draw **dotted, fading zones of influence/trade** along the Gulf of Thailand coast to the Thai‑Malay isthmus (Dunsun), labelled "influence claimed by Chinese records".
- Animate expansion and contraction as **opacity changes, not hard lines**.
- **Never** colour all of modern Cambodia, Thailand and southern Viet Nam as "Funan territory".
- Avoid modern borders entirely, or show them faint and grey with a caption "modern borders for reference".
- For Chenla, use a separate soft zone in the middle Mekong (Wat Phu – Sambor Prei Kuk), overlapping and replacing Funan's glow in the 7th c.
- Coastline: the c. 500 CE delta coast lay further inland than today in places (Malleret's Nền Chùa was near the old shore). If possible, push the coast slightly inland around the Cà Mau/Rạch Giá area and label it "approximate ancient coast".

## 16. DO NOT SAY list

1. **"Funan was a Khmer empire that ruled all of Cambodia (and Thailand/Viet Nam)."** Its extent is unknown; its language is debated; it may have been a network of port polities.
2. **Exact borders, "territory of 5–6000 li" converted into km on a map, or a list of conquered countries placed as filled areas.**
3. **"The Chinese records tell of Kaundinya and Princess Soma."** The Chinese name the pair *Hun Tian & Liu Ye*. **Somā** comes from the **Cham inscription C.96 (658 CE)** and Angkorian inscriptions; in the 10th‑c. Khmer texts she is the daughter of the Moon, not a nāga.
4. **"Preah Thong and Neang Neak founded Funan" / "Chinese envoys heard the Preah Thong story."** This is a later Khmer folk legend; Vickery dates it post‑Angkor.
5. **"Hun Tian was an Indian brahmin from India"** as fact. Not in the Chinese texts (only the 10th‑c. *Taiping yulan* says brahmin).
6. **"The Antoninus Pius coin proves Romans visited Oc Eo"** or "Roman traders lived in Funan". The pieces (probably locally made pendants) travelled via Indian and Southeast Asian intermediaries. Also avoid the unverified "minted in 152 CE".
7. **"Funan was founded in 68 CE" (or any exact founding date)**, and the Wikipedia‑style list "Soma 180–190, Kaundinya 190–198…". These are invented precision.
8. **"Funan means 'mountain'"** as certain fact. Say "is traditionally linked to".
9. **"Queen Kulaprabhāvatī ruled Funan 514–517"**, or "Rudravarman murdered Guṇavarman" as fact. These are hypotheses at most.
10. **"Fan Shiman ruled as far as Nha Trang" (Śrī Māra of Võ Cạnh = Fan Shiman).** Rejected.
11. **"Phnom Da statues are definitely Funan art of Rudravarman's reign."** Dating is debated (6th vs 7th c.).
12. **"Funan was conquered and destroyed by Chenla in [exact year]."** The sources conflict (Citrasena vs Īśānavarman); many historians see gradual change; "Funan" embassies continue to 649.
13. **"The people of Funan were ugly / black / naked"** as a description. These are prejudiced outsider statements; frame them as such or omit.
14. **"Oc Eo is in Cambodia"**, or contested‑territory framing. It is in An Giang, Viet Nam; describe neutrally. Also do not call it a UNESCO World Heritage Site (nominated; decision pending 2027).
15. **"The capital was Vyādhapura at Ba Phnom" / "Nafuna was Angkor Borei/Oc Eo"** as fact. These are hypotheses.
16. **"≈200 km of canals"** or other exact network totals. Unverified.
17. **"Funan spoke Khmer" or "Funan spoke Malay"** as fact. Unknown.
18. **"Indian colonists founded Funan"**. This is the old colonial‑era theory; present it only as one historical view.
19. **"The 'Funan Hall' was a Funan embassy building"** — it was one of the translation venues in Jiankang named 扶南館; exact function unclear. Keep the wording neutral.
20. Avoid modern titles for ancient rulers (e.g. **ព្រះបាទ "Preah Bat" Kaundinya**). Vickery: the title was unknown in Funan. Use "king" / «ស្តេច» or the name alone.
